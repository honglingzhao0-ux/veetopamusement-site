# FOB vs CIF for Amusement Equipment: Which Term to Use

Shipping terms look like a detail until the invoice arrives. At that point the difference between FOB and CIF on a single container of amusement equipment can be larger than the discount you spent three weeks negotiating, and the difference is often made up of charges that nobody told you were yours.

This guide explains where the responsibility boundary actually sits for each term, why the choice matters more for amusement machines than for most other cargo, and how to compare two quotations written on different terms.

## What FOB and CIF actually mean

Both terms place the transfer of risk at the same moment: **when the goods are on board the vessel at the port of shipment.** This is the detail most buyers get wrong. Buyers routinely assume CIF shifts risk to the destination port, because they associate CIF with the arrival port in the term name. It does not. The difference between the two terms is not risk, it is cost and paperwork.

**FOB (Free on Board)** means the seller covers everything up to and including loading the goods on board the vessel at the named port of shipment. From that point the buyer pays ocean freight, marine insurance, destination port charges, customs clearance, import duty and inland delivery.

**CIF (Cost, Insurance and Freight)** means the seller's price includes the cost of the goods, the ocean freight to the named destination port, and marine insurance. The seller arranges and pays for freight and insurance. The buyer still pays destination port handling charges, customs clearance, import duty and inland delivery, and still owns the risk from the moment of loading.

| Item | FOB | CIF |
|---|---|---|
| Goods price | Seller | Seller |
| Export clearance and charges | Seller | Seller |
| Loading on board at origin port | Seller | Seller |
| Ocean freight to destination | Buyer | Seller |
| Marine insurance | Buyer | Seller (minimum ICC C, 110% of invoice) |
| Risk transfer point | On board at origin | On board at origin |
| Destination terminal handling (THC) | Buyer | Buyer |
| Import customs clearance and duty | Buyer | Buyer |
| Inland haulage from port | Buyer | Buyer |
| Demurrage and detention | Buyer | Buyer |

## Why the choice matters more for amusement equipment

Amusement machines are a genuinely awkward cargo category, and the two properties that make them awkward both push in the same direction.

**First, low value density.** A 40HQ offers around 76 cubic metres of internal volume, but a full-size claw crane at W78 x D85 x H190 cm occupies roughly 1.26 cubic metres of that space for a machine whose retail value is a fraction of the freight on a multi-thousand-dollar shipping line. Volume-heavy, value-light cargo means freight is a large percentage of landed cost. If freight is 5% of cargo value for a normal export, it can easily be 12 to 20% for amusement equipment.

**Second, oversize and heavy handling.** Machines are crated in plywood and timber, they arrive as general cargo with forklift and crane requirements, and they are frequently classified as out-of-gauge or heavy cargo at destination. The charges that follow are not small. Destination terminal handling, container seal and documentation fees, and demurrage when a container sits at the port because your customs broker did not clear it can each exceed the ocean freight line on a single container.

The result is that the FOB-to-CIF gap, which is just freight plus insurance, is not the whole story. The difference in **who carries the risk of getting these charges wrong** is worth as much as the difference in the price itself.

## Where the destination port charges actually land

This is where most importers get caught, and it is worth stating plainly: **CIF does not include destination port charges.** Sellers who quote CIF are not absorbing the THC, the documentation fee, the seal fee, or customs clearance. Those arrive at destination and are payable by the buyer regardless of the term used.

| Charge | Typical treatment | Rough scale for a 40HQ |
|---|---|---|
| Ocean freight Guangzhou to Rotterdam / Dubai / Los Angeles | Included in CIF, excluded from FOB | 1,200 - 3,200 USD depending on route and season |
| Marine insurance, all-risks, 110% of invoice | Included in CIF at a minimum level | 0.3 - 0.8% of insured value |
| Destination terminal handling charge (THC) | Buyer, both terms | 250 - 600 USD |
| Documentation and seal fees | Buyer, both terms | 40 - 150 USD |
| Customs clearance and entry fees | Buyer, both terms | 100 - 350 USD |
| Import duty | Buyer, both terms | 0 - 6% depending on tariff classification |
| Import VAT (EU) or GST (GCC) | Buyer, both terms | 19 - 21% in the EU on the CIF value plus duty |
| Inland haulage, port to your warehouse | Buyer, both terms | Highly local, usually 150 - 600 USD |
| Demurrage and detention | Buyer, both terms | Charged per container per day if the container is not picked up |

**The VAT point deserves emphasis for European buyers.** In the EU, import VAT is calculated on the customs value plus duty plus freight and insurance to the destination port. That means the CIF price is literally the base on which your biggest single import cost is computed, while under FOB the same figure depends on what your forwarder charges you. The two terms can produce a different tax base on the same physical shipment.

**The numbers in the table are planning estimates only.** Actual freight moves with bunker surcharge, container availability, peak season and route disruption, so treat them as a structure to work from and replace every line with a live quote before you commit.

## A worked comparison for one 40HQ

Take a mixed container of arcade and redemption machines with a factory value of 40,000 USD, shipping from Guangzhou to Rotterdam, and assume 26 units of mixed cabinets.

### On FOB at 40,000 USD

- Supplier arranges export clearance and loading
- Buyer books ocean freight: estimated 2,200 - 2,800 USD
- Buyer arranges insurance at 0.4% of the invoice plus 10%: approximately 160 USD
- Buyer pays destination THC, documentation and seal: approximately 450 - 700 USD
- Buyer pays customs clearance, duty and import VAT at 19% on a base of roughly 44,000 USD: approximately 8,360 USD
- **Total buyer cost above the goods: approximately 11,200 - 12,000 USD**

### On CIF at approximately 42,600 USD

- Seller arranges freight and minimum insurance, delivers to Rotterdam
- Buyer still pays THC, documentation and seal: approximately 450 - 700 USD
- Buyer still pays clearance, duty and import VAT at 19% on a CIF base of 42,600 USD: approximately 8,100 USD
- **Total buyer cost above the goods: approximately 8,550 - 8,800 USD**

The difference is the freight and insurance the seller carries on your behalf, less the fact that you no longer hold an uninsured risk between the origin port and your warehouse door. In this example the saving is around 2,400 to 2,800 USD on a single container, or roughly 90 to 110 USD per machine.

That per-machine figure is the number to compare against any unit price difference between two suppliers. A quotation that is 3% cheaper on FOB can still be 2% more expensive on CIF. **Never compare an FOB price with a CIF price directly.**

## When to choose FOB and when to choose CIF

| Your situation | Better term | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| You have a freight forwarder you trust and a framework rate | FOB | You control the booking, the container and the sailing schedule |
| You are a first-time importer without a forwarder relationship | CIF | One party handles freight and insurance; fewer moving parts to get wrong |
| You want to lock the total ocean freight cost before you pay the balance | CIF | Freight is fixed in the seller's price, so the invoice is predictable |
| Your machines are heavy, oversized or need special handling | FOB, with a good forwarder | You can specify a carrier and equipment that can actually handle the cargo |
| You are consolidating several suppliers into one container | FOB | Consolidation is easier when you control the cut-off times and the stuffing plan |
| You are shipping to a country with a complex customs regime | CIF | The seller's familiarity with the destination paperwork is worth something |
| You are placing a large single order and want a single point of contact | CIF | One invoice, one claim path |

## The three mistakes that cost money

**Comparing quotations on different terms.** This is the big one, and it is easy to do by accident when a buyer's inbox contains one FOB price from one supplier and one CIF price from another. Normalise every quotation to the same term before you compare, or you will draw the wrong conclusion about which supplier is cheaper.

**Under-insuring the cargo.** Marine insurance on a CIF term is frequently written at the minimum level, which covers the named perils only. Amusement machines crated in timber, shipped in a damp container and handled by forklifts several times, have a real exposure to crushing, puncture and moisture damage that named-perils cover does not address. Ask for the policy wording and confirm the cover is all-risks with damage from collision and moisture included. The standard minimum is 110% of the invoice value, and for a shipment where the goods sit in a warehouse until your venue opens, 110% of goods value is not enough protection.

**Letting the container sit.** Demurrage is charged per container per day and it starts the moment the vessel discharges, regardless of what your paperwork is doing. The most common cause is a customs broker who needs a document you did not send. Ask your forwarder for a document checklist before the container sails, and make sure your broker has it before the vessel arrives.

## Incoterms quick reference

| Term | Seller covers | Buyer covers | Risk transfers |
|---|---|---|---|
| EXW (Ex Works) | Goods at the factory gate | Everything, including export clearance | At the factory gate |
| FOB (Free on Board) | Goods, export clearance, loading on board | Freight, insurance, destination, duty | On board at origin |
| CFR (Cost and Freight) | Goods, export clearance, freight to destination port | Insurance, destination charges, duty | On board at origin |
| CIF (Cost, Insurance and Freight) | Goods, export clearance, freight, minimum insurance | Destination charges, clearance, duty | On board at origin |
| DAP (Delivered at Place) | Goods, export clearance, freight, insurance, delivery to named address | Import clearance, duty, unloading | At the named delivery address |
| DDP (Delivered Duty Paid) | Everything including import duty and delivery | Almost nothing | At the named delivery address |

EXW and DDP are the two ends of the spectrum, and DDP in particular should be treated with care by a first-time importer, because the seller takes on responsibility for import clearance in a jurisdiction you know better than they do.

## What to ask your supplier

Ask specifically: what is the FOB price and the CIF price for the same configuration, what insurance cover is included in the CIF price and which policy it is written on, what destination charges are excluded from CIF, what documents are issued and when, and what the production and dispatch schedule looks like against the sailing you need. Then confirm the packing specification, because a machine crated for a 40HQ and a machine crated for a flat rack are different products with different costs.
